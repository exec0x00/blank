1Testing homogeneity: the scaling test

Definitions

Homogeneous function (scaling form). u(x,y) is homogeneous of degree n if u(λx,λy)=λnu(x,y). Here n can be positive, negative or even zero. S006·5 · T006b 00:00:52 · T006b 00:01:11
Not homogeneous. u is not homogeneous when u(λx,λy)=λnu(x,y) for every n: no single power of λ can be taken out. S006·8 · T006b 00:04:59

Key points

  1. Write u(λx,λy), take the highest power of λ out of the numerator and the denominator, and cancel. ESA ★★ S006·5 · T006b 00:02:17
  2. A λ inside a ratio cancels: sin−1λxλy​=sin−1xy​, so functions of y/x add degree 0. ESA ★★ S006·6 · T006b 00:03:34
  3. eλx cannot be written as λnex, so exponentials of x or y alone break homogeneity (slide 006·8). ESA ★★ S006·8 · T006b 00:04:40
  4. Simplify first when you can: slide 006·5 is (x2+y2)2(x2+y2)2​=1 and slide 006·7 is (x+y)3xy(x+y)​=(x+y)2xy​; both have degree 0. ESA ★ added for clarity S006·5 · S006·7

Why?

Why does slide 006·8 fail the test?
u(λx,λy)=λ(x−y)eλx−eλy​. The denominator gives λ−1, but eλx−eλy is not a power of λ times ex−ey. Figure G1.1 shows the ratio is not a straight line on log-log axes.

3When u itself is homogeneous: first and second order

Statements in the class material

“By Euler's theorem for a homogeneous function of degree n: x2uxx​+2xyuxy​+y2uyy​=n(n−1)u.” S006·11

Key points

  1. First prove that u is homogeneous and find n; then write both answers without differentiating. ESA ★★★ T006b 00:06:11 · T006b 00:06:45
  2. Give the answer in terms of x and y too: −u=−x2+y2x−y​ and 2u=x2+y22(x−y)​. ESA ★★ S006·10 · S006·11
  3. For a sum of parts with different degrees, apply the corollary part by part: n1​(n1​−1)u1​+n2​(n2​−1)u2​. ESA ★★★ added for clarity ESA Jan-24 s3 1c · CW U1 P22

Exam traps

Exam trap. n=−1 gives n(n−1)=(−1)(−2)=+2, not −2: the second-order answer on slide 006·11 is +2u. added for clarity S006·11

4When f(u) is homogeneous: the extension

Definitions

Extension function g(u). g(u)=nf′(u)f(u)​, where f(u) is homogeneous of degree n in x and y. S006·13 · S006·15 · S006·17

Statements in the class material

“For f(u) a homogeneous function of degree n: xux​+yuy​=n(f′(u)f(u)​)=g(u), and x2uxx​+2xyuxy​+y2uyy​=g(u)[g′(u)−1].” S006·13 · S006·15 · S006·17

Key points

  1. When u is sin−1, tan−1, log or e of something, apply the inverse function to both sides: that is f(u). ESA ★★★ T006b 00:08:20 · T006b 00:09:55 · T006b 00:11:40
  2. The degree belongs to f(u), found by the scaling test on f(u) (λ4 on slide 006·12, λ on 006·14, λ1/2 on 006·16). ESA ★★★ S006·12 · S006·14 · S006·16
  3. “Whenever you have a complicated function, you can always use Euler's theorem if you feel that the function is homogeneous” — the lecture's closing advice for part I. ESA ★★ T006a 00:08:53
  4. Three variables work the same way: on slide 006·4, sinu has degree −3, so xux​+yuy​+zuz​=−3tanu. ESA ★★ S006·4 · T006a 00:06:54

Why?

Why does the log case give −1 for the second order?
f(u)=eu gives g=neu/eu=n, a constant. So g′=0 and g(g′−1)=−n; slide 006·14 has n=1.
S006·15
Why can't Euler's theorem be applied to u directly on slides 006·12–17?
u is not homogeneous: for u=logx+yx2+y2​, u(λx,λy)=logλ+u, which is a shift, not a factor λn.

Exam traps

Exam trap. For u=e(…) the function is f(u)=logu, so f′=1/u and f/f′=ulogu: the answer is 4ulogu, not 4logu. added for clarity S006·13
Exam trap. In the second order use g′(u), the derivative of g with respect to u, not f′(u): for g=21​tanu, g′=21​sec2u. S006·17 · T006b 00:12:42
