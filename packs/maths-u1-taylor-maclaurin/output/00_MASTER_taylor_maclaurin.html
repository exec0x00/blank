1Why Taylor's and Maclaurin's series?

A complicated function is hard to evaluate, integrate or take limits of; a polynomial is easy. Taylor's series writes a function as a polynomial (of infinitely many terms) around a point, and a few terms are usually enough. This is how values such as etan33​, integrals such as ∫01​sin(x2)dx and limits such as limx3sinx−x​ are handled, and it has many applications in physics and engineering.

Important points and why

  1. Once a function is a polynomial, you can differentiate, integrate, find limits and study how it changes. ESA ★ T007 00:06:50

Applications (one variable): polynomials for complicated functions, definite integrals with no closed form, asymptotic behaviour, growth of functions, differential equations, the Lorentz factor, E=mc2 at low speed, sinθ≈θ for a pendulum, power-flow analysis, generating functions.   3. Applications (two variables): optimization (a linear or quadratic model), accurate distance measurement with the sine law, variance of complicated configurations, visualizing complicated functions. ESA ★ S007·12 · T007 00:21:22
  4. A few terms are often enough: four terms of the sine series already follow sinx closely near 0, and ten terms overlap it (Figure G1.1). ESA ★ S007·8 · T007 00:15:24 · T007 00:16:10

Why does a calculator need series at all?
It can only add and multiply. A polynomial needs nothing else, so a function such as etanx is evaluated through polynomial approximations.

2Taylor's and Maclaurin's series in one variable

If f has continuous derivatives, f(x)=f(a)+(x−a)f′(a)+2!(x−a)2​f′′(a)+… about x=a; about the origin this is Maclaurin's series. The first two terms are the tangent line. The standard series for (1+x)n, ex, log(1+x), sinx, cosx, sinhx, coshx, tan−1x, sin−1x and cos−1x are on slide 007·5. This one-variable part is a self-learning component of the syllabus.

Key terms and definitions

Taylor's series (one variable). If a function f(x) has continuous derivatives of order (n+1), then this function can be expressed as a polynomial at a point x=a in the form f(x)=f(a)+(x−a)f′(a)+2!(x−a)2​f′′(a)+⋯+n!(x−a)n​f(n)(a)+… S007·4 · T007 00:04:19
Maclaurin's series (one variable). If the function is expanded about the origin we get Maclaurin's series: f(x)=f(0)+xf′(0)+2!x2​f′′(0)+⋯+n!xn​f(n)(0)+…; it is a particular case of Taylor's series. S007·4 · T007 00:06:17

Important points and why

  1. Keep only the first two terms of Taylor's series and you get f(x)≈f(a)+(x−a)f′(a): the tangent line, i.e. the derivative as a slope (Figure G2.1). ESA ★ T007 00:05:26 · T007 00:05:43
  2. To get a standard series, differentiate repeatedly and put x=0: every derivative of ex is ex, which is 1 at the origin. ESA ★ T007 00:09:10 · T007 00:09:44
  3. Substitute into a known series rather than differentiating: sin(x2)=x2−3!x6​+5!x10​−…. ESA ★★ T007 00:11:51
  4. No Maclaurin series exists if f or a derivative is infinite at 0: 1/x, logx, x​. ESA ★ T007 00:10:18
When is Maclaurin's series valid?
f must have derivatives of every order at 0 (so none is infinite there), and the series must converge to f. ex, sinx, cosx converge for every x; log(1+x) and (1+x)n only for ∣x∣<1.

3Taylor's expansion of a function of two variables

About a point (a,b): f(x,y)=f(a,b)+1!1​{(x−a)fx​+(y−b)fy​}+2!1​{(x−a)2fxx​+2(x−a)(y−b)fxy​+(y−b)2fyy​}+…, with every derivative evaluated at (a,b). The coefficients 1, 2, 1 (and 1, 3, 3, 1 at third order) are binomial, because the mixed partials are equal. ESA questions give the point and the degree.

Key terms and definitions

Taylor polynomial of degree n. The terms of Taylor's series up to total degree n in (x−a) and (y−b); “expand up to second (third) degree terms” means stop there. Degree 1 is the tangent plane. added for clarity S007·11 · T007 00:20:13

4Maclaurin's expansion of a function of two variables

Put (a,b)=(0,0): f(x,y)=f(0,0)+1!1​{xfx​+yfy​}+2!1​{x2fxx​+2xyfxy​+y2fyy​}+… at the origin. fx​ goes with x, fy​ with y, fxx​ with x2, fxy​ with 2xy, fyy​ with y2. When f is a function of x+2y or x−y, the one-variable series gives the answer as a check.

Eight past ESA papers were checked: UE22MA141A Dec 2023, Jul 2023 and May 2024, and UE23MA141A Jan 2024 sets 1–4 and June 2024. A Taylor or Maclaurin expansion in two variables is asked in six of them, always as a 6–7 mark Q1 part. About a point (a,b): Dec 2023 Q1.b (sinxsiny, third degree), Jul 2023 Q1.b (the same function, second order) and May 2024 Q1.b (excosy about (1,π/4)). About the origin: Jan 2024 set 1 Q1.b (log(1+x−y)), set 2 Q1.b (eaxsinby) and June 2024 Q1.c (sin(x+2y)). Four of the six ask for third-degree terms, which the slides do not write out. Jan 2024 sets 3 and 4 have no Taylor question. One-variable series and the applications were never asked.

Known gaps, conflicts and corrections

  1. Slide 007·9's second-order term is garbled in the rendering. It was rebuilt from the transcript (T007 00:18:45) and the Maclaurin form on slide 007·10: 2!1​{(x−a)2fxx​+2(x−a)(y−b)fxy​+(y−b)2fyy​} at (a,b).
  2. The slides stop at second order. Four of the six ESA questions ask for third-degree terms, so the third-order term 3!1​{h3fxxx​+3h2kfxxy​+3hk2fxyy​+k3fyyy​} and the operator form are added for clarity.
  3. Slide 007·3 asks for limx→0​x4sinx−x​. That limit does not exist (it behaves like −6x1​); with x3 in the denominator the limit is −61​, worked as W1.2.
  4. Slide 007·3's integrals (∫ex2dx, ∫cos(x2)dx) are partly lost in the rendering; the lecture uses ∫01​sin(x2)dx instead (T007 00:11:51), worked as W1.1.
  5. Slide 007·7 says “E=mc2 is an approximation for low velocities”. More precisely, the Maclaurin expansion of the relativistic energy γmc2 gives mc2+21​mv2 for v≪c (added for clarity).
  6. Slide 007·8's graphs of sinx and its Maclaurin polynomials have no axis labels; they are redrawn as Figure G1.1.
  7. Slide 007·11 does not state the expansion point. Its polynomials 1+x+y and 1+x+xy+2y2​ are the Taylor polynomials of xey+1 about (1,0); the lecture's guess that “maybe they have used the Maclaurin series” (T007 00:20:32) is wrong: about the origin they would be 1+x and 1+x+xy (sympy).
  8. Slide 007·12 is titled “Applications of Euler's Theorem of a multivariate function”; its content is the applications of Taylor's series (a copy error).
  9. The lecture says 1+2+22+23+… “gives −1” (T007 00:07:52). The series diverges: 1−x1​=1+x+x2+… holds only for ∣x∣<1. Corrected as an exam trap.
  10. The lecture's condition “f(a) cannot tend to infinity” (T007 00:10:01) is incomplete: f needs derivatives of every order at the point, and the series must converge to f. The precise condition is added for clarity.
  11. The lecture's picture of a calculator summing Maclaurin series is a simplification; real calculators use several polynomial and shift-and-add methods. It is kept as the lecture's motivation.
  12. Class-work problem 26 prints tan−1(0.9/1.1)≈0.6904. The second-order Taylor value is 0.6854 (exact 0.6857).
  13. Class 008 (“Problems on Taylor's and Maclaurin's series”) is a separate session and is not included.
