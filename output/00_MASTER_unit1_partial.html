T3 Born-Oppenheimer approximation

Key points

  • Total molecular energy = translational + rotational + vibrational + electronic energies [S003]
  • Translational energy is negligibly small, so E = Erot + Evib + Eelec [S003]
  • Electronic, vibrational and rotational energies are treated as independent, because electronic levels are much farther apart than vibrational levels, which are much farther apart than rotational levels: Erot « Evib « Eelec [S003]
  • Transition energy map: rotational transitions are microwave, vibrational are infrared, electronic are optical or UV [S003, S007]
E = E_trans + E_rot + E_vib + E_elec   (slide 003, equation not in text; standard form)

T4 Rotational (microwave) spectroscopy

Key points

  • Gross selection rule: molecule must have a permanent dipole moment. HCl and CO are microwave active; H2, Cl2 and CO2 are not [S004 slides 3, 5]
  • Model: rigid rotor for a heteronuclear diatomic, masses m1, m2 joined by a rod of length r0 = r1 + r2, rotating about the centre of mass [S004 slide 6]
  • Rotational quantum number J = 0, 1, 2, 3 … (integers from zero upwards) [S004 slide 8]
  • Selection rule: ΔJ = ±1 [S004 slide 10]
  • Rotational energy levels in cm-1, with rotational constant B: εJ = BJ(J+1). Levels: J=0 gives 0, J=1 gives 2B, J=2 gives 6B, J=3 gives 12B [S004 slide 9]
  • Line positions: the transition J → J+1 absorbs at Δε = 2B(J+1). The first line is at 2B; spacing between lines is constant at 2B: 2B, 4B, 6B, 8B, 10B [S004 slide 10-11]
  • Information from the spectrum: B from the line spacing, then I = μr02 gives the bond length r0 [S004 final slide]
  • Higher rotational levels are already populated at room temperature (intensity pattern in the spectrum) [S004]
εJ = BJ(J+1) cm-1    Not visually verified (slide 8 equation missing in text)
I = μ r02,   μ = m1m2/(m1+m2)    Not visually verified (slides 7 and final slide)
Δε(J→J+1) = 2B(J+1) cm-1    Not visually verified
Exam trap: the first rotational line is at 2B, not B. J starts at 0.

T5 Vibrational spectroscopy: simple harmonic oscillator (SHO)

Key points

  • Gross selection rule: the vibration must change the dipole moment (IR active) [S005]
  • CO2: symmetric stretch is IR inactive (no dipole change); asymmetric stretch and bending are IR active [S005]
  • Bond modelled as a spring obeying Hooke's law; the potential energy curve is parabolic [S005]
  • Energy levels are equally spaced; zero-point energy ε0 is non-zero [S005]
  • Selection rule Δv = ±1; so only one line is seen in the SHO spectrum [S005]
  • From the spectrum the oscillation wavenumber is read, and the force constant k (bond strength) follows from it [S005]
  • Every bond has a unique natural frequency, so each molecule's IR spectrum is unique (a fingerprint) [S005]
νosc = (1/2π)√(k/μ) Hz    Not visually verified (slide 005 equation missing in text)
εv = (v + ½) ν̄osc cm-1,   ε0 = ½ν̄osc    Not visually verified

T6 Vibrational spectroscopy: anharmonic oscillator

Key points

  • Real bonds are anharmonic: stretched far enough, the bond breaks and the molecule dissociates [S006]
  • Morse potential: PE = De[1 − exp{a(r0 − r)}]2, where De is the depth of the PE curve and a is a constant [S006]
  • Anharmonicity constant xe is small and positive for bond stretching [S006]
  • Energy spacing decreases as v increases; levels converge toward the dissociation limit [S006]
  • Selection rule: Δv = ±1, ±2, ±3 … (overtones allowed) [S006]
  • Most transitions start from v=0 (most populated). Fundamental: 0→1; first overtone: 0→2; second overtone: 0→3 [S006]
  • Hot bands: at higher temperature, transitions from v=1 are also seen; the 1→2 hot band lies to the left of the fundamental [S006]
εv = (v+½)ω̄e − (v+½)2ω̄exe cm-1    Not visually verified
Fundamental = ω̄e(1 − 2xe);   First overtone = 2ω̄e(1 − 3xe)    Not visually verified (from brief table, check against deck 006 slides 7 and 9)

T7 Electronic spectroscopy and the Franck-Condon principle

Key points

  • HOMO → LUMO transitions in the UV-Visible region: σ→σ*, π→π*, n→&pi* [S007]
  • All molecules can undergo electronic transitions (unlike IR or microwave) [S007]
  • Electronic transitions carry vibrational structure (coarse structure) and rotational structure (fine structure) [S007]
  • No selection rule restricts the vibrational level reached during an electronic transition [S007]
  • Most transitions start from v″=0 (most populated). Lines are labelled (v′, v″); the set of lines is a progression; lines converge at high energy, showing anharmonicity in the excited state [S007]
  • Franck-Condon principle (slide wording): “An electronic transition takes place so rapidly that a vibrating molecule does not change its internuclear distance appreciably during the transition.” Electronic transitions are therefore vertical [S007]
  • Three cases [S007]:
    • re″ = re″″: strongest line is (0,0); intensity falls for the rest
    • re″ > re″″ (a little greater): strongest line is (v′, 0)
    • re″ » re″″ (considerably greater): the molecule passes the dissociation energy, giving a continuum (kinetic energy of separated atoms is not quantised)
  • Intensity is highest from the centre of v″″=0, where the probability of finding the nuclei is maximal [S007]
Note on the transcript: the class 007 transcript spells the principle "Frank Condon"; the correct name is Franck-Condon.

T8 Phase, component, degrees of freedom and the phase rule

Definitions

  • Phase: any homogeneous, physically distinct part of a system, bounded by a surface and mechanically separable. Gas: P = 1 (gases are completely miscible). Liquid: P = number of layers if immiscible, 1 if miscible. Solid: each solid is a separate phase; each polymorph is a separate phase; P = 1 for a solid solution [S008]
  • Component: the smallest number of independently varying chemical constituents needed to express the composition of every phase [S008]
  • Degrees of freedom (variance) F: the minimum number of intensive variables (T, P, concentration) that must be fixed to define the system, equivalently the number that can vary without changing the number of phases [S008]
  • Chemical potential: phases in equilibrium have equal chemical potential for each component [S009]

Statement of the phase rule

F = C − P + 2   (Gibbs, 1874; valid when equilibrium is not influenced by gravity, electric or magnetic fields) [S008]

T9 Derivation of the phase rule; one-component water diagram

Derivation (slide 009)

  • Variables: each of the P phases needs T, P and (C−1) mole fractions, so total variables = P(C−1) + 2 [S009]
  • Equilibrium constraints: chemical potential of each component equal across the phases, giving (P−1) equations per component, so C(P−1) in total [S009]
  • F = variables − constraints = P(C−1) + 2 − C(P−1) = C − P + 2 [S009]

One-component system (water)

  • F = 3 − P [S009]
  • One phase: F = 2, an area [S009]
  • Two phases: F = 1, a line [S009]
  • Three phases: F = 0, a point (triple point; for water 0.0098 °C and 4.58 mm Hg, as stated on slide 008) [S008, S009]
  • High pressure, low temperature: solid. High temperature, low pressure: vapour [S009]

T11 Reduced phase rule and the Pb-Ag eutectic system

Key points (slide 010)

  • For two components with the full rule, F = 4 − P; at minimum P = 1, F = 3, which needs 3-D space, so it cannot be drawn on paper [S010]
  • Measurements are usually at constant (atmospheric) pressure, which removes one degree of freedom: F = C − P + 1, the reduced (condensed) phase rule. Valid for condensed systems with no gas phase [S010]
  • Pb–Ag: liquid miscible in all proportions; solid completely immiscible [S010]
  • Melting points: Pb 327 °C, Ag 961 °C; eutectic temperature 303 °C at 2.6 % Ag (97.4 % Pb) [S010]
  • Cooling path: first solid Pb forms at the Pb freezing curve; the melt becomes richer in Ag and moves along curve BO. At point O, solid Ag also separates; three phases (solid Ag, solid Pb, melt) coexist, F = 0 [S010]
  • Areas: above BOA liquid melt; AOD solid Ag + liquid; BOC solid Pb + liquid; below COD solid Ag + solid Pb [S010]
Labelling conflict: the slide names A = 100 % Pb and B = 100 % Ag, but its text also describes curve BO as the freezing curve of Pb. Use the lettering your faculty uses in exams.
